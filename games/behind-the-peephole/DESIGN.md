# Behind the Peephole: design

## 1. Summary

You take a night job behind the fourth floor of an old river hotel and watch six guests come back
from work you will never see, through peepholes fitted backwards into six sealed valet doors. Over
seven nights you spend a fixed number of hours across six doors, write down who did what from
what they carry home, and decide on the last night what to do with the log you now know was the
product all along.

## 2. Why this game

| | Behind the Peephole | Man Eater | Missing Campsites |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Core verb | Watch | Eat | Compare |
| Where you are | One service passage; you may never enter the rooms you watch | Anywhere on the base, at a cost | A campground on foot |
| What is investigated | Other people's hidden lives, with true answers that are scored | Your own body | Records against reality; nothing to deduce |
| Night cycle | A fixed budget of hours spent across six doors | A day of duties, then a night of choices | Three days on a shrinking map |
| Main pressure | Missing one door to watch another; being seen watching | Hunger and cold | Things vanishing |
| Endings decided by | Accuracy of your statements plus one choice on the last night | A ledger of meals and broken rules | What and whom you carried |

What no other game in the collection does: the whole of the story happens off-screen, and the
player builds it from the few minutes after each guest comes home. The play is attention itself:
where you put your eye, and for how long, before the person on the other side feels it.

**The author's ending.** The pitch has none. All four endings are proposals and none carries
`as_written`. The craft rules of the vault's horror-prose mode still apply: every ending keeps a
cost, and none is a tidy rescue. Each ending is one of the allowed shapes: handoff, no-return,
irreversible knowledge, complicit.

The annotation notes that the pitch "never settles on a protagonist" and that its point of view
"is the audience's inference". The game takes that literally: the protagonist has no face, no
name and no spoken line. The player is the inference.

## 4. Investment

**The hook in the first 5 minutes.** Rain in a staff alley at 22:35. The night porter takes you up
the service stair and unlocks a passage nobody has used in years: bottle-green wallpaper, a
runner worn to the boards, and six small brass roses at eye height, each a coin of warm light.
Before anyone explains the job, the player puts an eye to the sixth rose (the passage scene ends
on a push-in to it). Room 406 is empty. A television plays the night river report to nobody. On
the window sill sits one folded paper boat. Then a bell rings four times somewhere behind you,
and a phone rings in the office. A calm voice says: "Log the returns. Only the returns." The
question: who lives in these rooms, and why is someone paying you to watch them?

**What the player comes to care about.** The six guests, through their private rituals, before any
of them is threatened. The game makes the care mechanical: rituals are one of the seven kinds of
evidence, and a ritual broken is the strongest clue in the game, so a player who wants to deduce
well has to learn each guest's habits by heart. By the end of night two the player has, in play:

| Guest | What the player has watched them do twice |
| --- | --- |
| 401, the mercenary | Lays out his kit in the same order, folds the bedspread back exactly a hand's width, then phones his daughter and reads her a chapter of a children's book. The player hears the story continue night by night. |
| 402, the whistleblower | Slides the door chain on seven times, turns a game show up loud, and whispers to a neighbour about her cat. |
| 403, the art thief | Puts on white cotton gloves before she touches anything, and paints a small watercolour of the river from her window each night. |
| 404, the journalist | Pours one minibar miniature into the sink and writes the day count of his sobriety on the hotel notepad: 400, 401. |
| 405, the hacker | Plays a game of chess with her grandmother over the phone, one move a night. |
| 406, the activist | Folds one paper boat and adds it to the row on the sill while the river report plays. |

The threat arrives on night three: the stranger gives the mercenary a photograph of the art thief
at her own door. From then on, the things the player learned to watch for are what break.

**The question that pulls them to the end.** At first: what are they really doing? From night
four: who else has done this job, and where do my logs go? On night seven: what will I do with
what I know, now that I know who it is for?

### Rules of the world

- Nothing is supernatural. The dread is human: being watched, being sold.
- **You never see a mission.** Every event outside the hotel is known only by its traces. No
  cutscene, flashback or call shows one.
- **You never enter a guest's room.** The valet doors are bolted from the room side. The player
  has no way through them and the game never offers one.
- **The lens works both ways.** When your eye is at a hole it blocks the light that leaks through
  it. From inside the room the brass rose goes dark. A guest who faces it can notice (section 12).
- **Every booking traces to one hand**: Wren Holdings, an account ending 0214.
- **The log is the product.** Each morning at 05:00 the watcher drops the night's pages down a
  brass chute. The chute ends in a steel box in the back stair. The stranger empties it at 2:14
  the next night. Wren sells the finished file.

## 8. Levels

### How a night runs (all nights)

- **The clock.** The shift runs from 23:00 to 05:00 (360 game minutes). In 3D and 2D the clock
  runs at 40× (1 real second is 40 game seconds), so a shift is 9 real minutes. A 1D page that
  keeps the world running (Night Patience, and Lockwork on nights three, four and six) runs the
  clock at 10×. The Statement, the Mosaic and the morning stop it.
- **Returns.** When a guest's key calls the lift, the bell board rings that room's number (four
  rings for 404). The bell is heard everywhere in the passage. The guest's main door opens 12 game
  minutes later (18 real seconds). Being at the lens when the door opens catches the threshold
  beat: hands, clothing and luggage, the densest clues of the night. The tables for nights one,
  two and seven give the bell's time ("Back"); those for nights three to six give the time the
  door opens ("Door opens").
- **Beats.** Each room has a timed script: up to 6 beats a night, each with a time window, a kind
  (one of the seven), and a place in the room. A beat is caught by holding the eye on it (section
  10). A caught beat becomes a clue card in the notebook.
- **The 2:14 knock.** Every night at 02:14 the stranger knocks three times (two quick, one slow)
  at one guest's main door. The knock is heard in the whole passage, panned to the right door
  within one door either way. It is logged in the `knocks` ledger with where the player was.

## 11. 1D games

Every 1D game is a full page. Text size, colour patterns and a pause apply to all of them.

### Lockwork (lock picking)

**Purpose in the story.** Every locked thing on the floor is a step closer to the people who hired
you: the drawer that holds your log, the stair door that hides where the log goes, the cabinet that
holds the last watcher's log, and the safe that holds who booked everyone. This is the only lock
game in the collection.

**Page layout.** A cutaway of the lock fills the centre: the plug and pins in brass and steel on
cream paper, like an engraving in a locksmith's manual. A tension gauge (0–100) runs down the left
edge. The tools lie along the bottom: tension wrench, hook pick, rake, jigglers or a file,
depending on the lock. Top right: spare picks (three at the start of the game, shared by every
lock). Top left: the clock, if the world is running.

| Appearance | Lock | World runs | Rules | Win | Failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `n1_drawer` | Pin tumbler, 3 pins | No | Hold tension in the band 20–70. Move the hook along the plug (left and right) and lift a pin (up). Only the binding pin moves; when it reaches the shear line under the right tension it clicks, the plug turns 1° and the next pin binds. Binding order is fixed per lock. Over 70 tension, pins jam; under 20, set pins drop. Lifting a pin past the shear line oversets it: release tension to reset. | All pins set; turn. | Three oversets: Lew opens it (`porter_helped`). |
| `n3_stair_lock` | Pin tumbler, 5 pins, 2 spool pins | Yes, 10× | Tension band 35–55. A spool pin gives a false set: the plug turns 5° and the pin pushes back. Ease tension to 30–35 while lifting it, then restore. Each overset after the second breaks a pick. | All five set. | Out of picks: the door stays locked tonight; the page offers it again on night six (`n6_stair_lock`, same rules, one pick restored). |
| `n4_lost_property` | Wafer lock, 5 wafers | Yes, 10× | Not picked but matched. A ring of 8 jiggler keys, each with five cut depths (1–4). Insert one and rock it: the lock tells you, by clicks, how many wafers sit at the right depth (0–5), not which. A blank and a file let you cut your own key, one depth per wafer. The cabinet's code is five depths. Ten insertions before Dora's 01:00 round (from 01:00 to 01:30 the page closes if she comes within 6 m). | A key that turns: five clicks. | Ten insertions used: try again after 01:30; the night's clock keeps running. |
| `n6_safe` | Safe dial, 3 numbers, 0–99 | Yes, 10× | Two ways. With patience card 9, dial 19–26–14 (left four turns, right three, left two) and turn the handle. Without it, manipulation: park the dial at a test number, then sweep to feel the two contact points; the page plots the gap between them on graph paper. Test at 0, 10, 20 … 90. The three narrowest gaps, refined in steps of 2, give the three numbers; try their six orders. | The handle turns. | No failure; the stranger's walk at 02:08 is the limit (section 8). |

**Difficulty curve.** 3 pins, wide band, no clock → 5 pins with spools, a narrow band, the clock

**Hints.** After 20 s without progress, the binding pin glows faintly (pin locks) or the next useful

### The Statement (logic grid)

**Purpose in the story.** Every morning Wren's log asks for a signed statement. It is the
player's deduction, and it is scored: correct rows lock in, and accuracy decides the endings.

**Page layout.** A double page of the logbook. Left page: the grid. Six room rows down the left.
Category A across the top (six columns), category B beside it (six columns), and the A × B grid
below A, as in a printed logic puzzle. Right page, top: the night's clue cards in a scrollable
stack, each a pencil sketch of what was seen with one line of handwriting; office clues in type.
Right page, bottom: the statement building itself in words as the grid fills ("Room 403 went to the
fish market and came back at 02:45."). A pen nib at the foot: "Sign".

**Rules.**

1. Click a cell to cycle blank → cross → tick. A tick crosses the rest of its row and column
   (can be turned off).
2. Clicking a clue card highlights the cells it bears on. It never fills them.
3. A row is complete when it has one tick in each category.
4. Sign once. Then each complete row is checked against the truth tables in section 8. A correct
   row is inked over and stamped; it carries to the Mosaic as a fact. A wrong or incomplete row
   stays in pencil and is struck through. The page does not say what the right answer was.
5. **Second look** (two per morning): pick a row; the page says whether its ticks agree with every
   clue card you hold. It does not say whether they are true. A false sign (section 12) makes an
   honest row disagree; the card that disagrees is shown, with its small tell.

### Night Patience (solitaire)

**Purpose in the story.** A game for the quiet before the first bell, with a worn deck from your
drawer. Someone keeps slipping extra cards into it. They are from Abel Fenn, the last watcher, and
they are the only voice in the game on your side.

**Page layout.** Green baize, seen from above. Six tableau columns across the middle, each headed
by a small brass room number (401–406). Four foundations top right. The stock and waste top left.
The spike (where slipped cards go) on the right edge. The clock bottom right, running at 10×.

**Rules.**

1. One 52-card deck plus that night's two slipped cards.
2. Deal six columns: column one has 1 card, column six has 6 (21 cards); the top card of each is
   face up. The rest (31) is the stock.
3. Build down in the tableau in alternating colours. Move any face-up run. Only a King fills an
   empty column.
4. Build up by suit on the foundations, Ace to King.
5. Turn the stock one card at a time. Once per pass, **peek**: see the next stock card without
   turning it.
6. A slipped card is placed once at a set depth: card A at stock position 6–10 (reached in almost
   any game), card B under the deepest face-down card of column six (reached only by clearing that
   column). When a slipped card would be shown, it lifts out, fills the page for 3 seconds, and
   goes on the spike. The spike keeps every card for the rest of the game; the player can read it
   in the office at any time.
7. **Finish.** A win (all 52 on the foundations), or the passes are used up with no legal move.
   The player can leave the page at any time; the deal stays as it was until 05:00.

### The Mosaic (link board)

**False signs.** A staged beat replaces one true beat. It looks like evidence and contradicts the
truth table. It has a tell: the guest does it facing the rose, and the clue card's sketch shows
their eyes on the viewer. A second look exposes the conflict.

**Exposure on the last night.** Each exposed guest keeps a towel on the rose all of night seven,
so there is less to watch before the knock. In the slide run, pages pushed under an exposed guest's
valet door come back out unread a moment later and do not count toward `pages_delivered`: that
guest already knows someone is in the passage and will not take paper from it. A player with three
or more exposed guests cannot reach Under the Door.

### Watching and attention

Every second at a lens is written to `watch_log` (night, room, from, to). At 02:14 on night seven
the game sets `most_watched_guest` to the room with the most total minutes. It names the guest
who does not come back in The Open Door.

### Staff

`seen_by_staff` counts the maid's sightings. At 2, Wren's next call keeps the player on the line:
the next shift starts at 23:15. At 3, the stair door is locked again (if open) until night six.

### The night strip

After each Statement, the morning scene shows a strip of the night: six rows (one per door), a
time axis 23:00–05:00, a dot where each beat was, filled if caught and hollow if missed. It never
says what a hollow dot was.

### Second shift (replay)

After any ending, a new run can start as a second shift. Clue cards caught in earlier runs are
kept in the notebook as carbon copies (grey) and can be used in Statements. The night strip shows
carbon beats as half-filled. Endings, choices and the Account start fresh. Replays do not count
toward the first playthrough's length.

**Final scenes.** `dawn_lobby`, `end_wrong_picture`. In the lobby, the guest named by

**Cost.** Someone who did not do what the file says pays for it, because the player said so.

**Last line.** The desk phone in the lobby rings and the night clerk holds it out toward the
mezzanine. Wren: "Thank you. That will be all."

### The Open Door

**Conditions.** The player opened the door to the stranger, or followed his steps to the stair.

```json
{"is": "final_choice", "eq": "answer_door"}
```

**Final scenes.** `n7_your_door`, `end_open_door`. The player walks with Fenn down the service

### The Next Watcher

**Conditions.** Everything else: in practice, the player kept logging (or the slide run failed to
reach four doors) with an accurate log.

```json
true
```

**Final scenes.** `dawn_lobby`, `end_next_watcher`. In the lobby, in the order the file predicted:
401 checks out with his kit bag; 406 is led out by police for the logger her sister planted; 402
leaves alone with her coat buttoned to the neck; 403 goes out through the service door with the
case. The last scene is 2D: the player's eye at a lens, tilting across a lit office, a figure at
the desk writing the log. The figure is the player, on next season's first night.

**Cost.** Every guest the player came to care about is sold, accurately.

**Last image.** The figure at the desk looks up at the rose.

### How a player reaches each one on purpose

| Ending | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Under the Door | Watch well and widely; open the safe on night six or collect four booker names; make the Wren – all six link; write a careful Account; avoid exposing three guests; slide the pages on night seven and run. |
| The Wrong Picture | Guess at the Statements, or get the Account wrong, and keep logging or slide the pages. |

green and brass, with the rooms behind the wall suggested only by light. 1D is paper: cream, pencil
grey, ink, baize, cork, brass; flat light; no depth.

## 15. Art direction

| Element | Direction |
| --- | --- |
| Palette | Bottle-green wallpaper, tobacco amber lamplight, brass, river grey. The lens view is the one place full warm colour lives. Red appears only as evidence (the clay, the rope burn, a cut palm). |

| Lighting | Practical sources only. Each room has its own lamp temperature (401 white, 402 the TV's blue, 403 a north-light daylight bulb, 404 yellow, 405 screen glow, 406 a bedside lamp with a red shade). The player learns the doors by their colour of light. |

| One look | All three layers are lit by the same practical lights and share the same textures: the 2D rooms are painted over renders of the 3D room set, and the 1D pages use the same paper and brass as the office. |

## 20. Risks and open questions

| Risk | Mitigation |
| --- | --- |
| Grids that cannot be solved, or can be solved without watching | A solver in CI for every night (milestone 3); a test that each grid is not solvable from office clues alone, except night one. |
| Missing things feels like failure, not choice | The night strip shows only that something happened, the second look rewards care, and the second shift keeps what was seen. |
| Too little happens through a lens | Every room has a beat at least every 20 game minutes while occupied; rituals fill gaps. |
| The Statement becomes a chore | Each night's grid asks a different question; the clue cards highlight their cells; seven grids, never more than 3 sub-grids. |
| The two Saelens sisters' reveal is spoiled by the surname | Surnames appear only on night five; before that they are room numbers and first names said once. |
| A pitch with so little text grows into something the author does not recognise | Section 21 lists every addition; the hidden plot can be replaced without touching the systems (the truth tables are data). |
| Ending conditions feel arbitrary | The file page after every ending shows the conditions and the run's values. |

**Questions only the author can answer.**

1. The pitch says "someone is watching them all". This design makes that someone Wren's file and,
   partly, the player, with the previous watcher as the stranger at 2:14. Is that the turn you
   meant, or did you have another watcher in mind?
2. The pitch has no protagonist. Is a faceless, silent watcher right for your book, or should the
   watcher have a life of their own that the guests' lives mirror?
3. Should one of the four endings be yours? If you have an ending for the novel, it would become
   the as-written ending and the other three would branch from it.
