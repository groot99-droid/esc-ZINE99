# Ten Game Adaptations


Ten of your works built out as games, each with a story, a gameplay design and a world script, so you can judge them side by side. Lines in quotation marks are verbatim from your text. Anything listed under "Added" is my proposal for the game, not your prose, and nothing here changes the originals.

| # | Game | Type of game | You play | Size | Main risk |

Each section below has the same three parts. **Story** is the narrative as the player meets it. **Gameplay** is what the player does. **World script** is the setting, its rules, its cast and the lines the game would speak.

## How every game is built

Two rules apply to all ten games. Each section ends with **Endings** and **Scenes and screens**, which apply them.

**Endings are conditional.** The ending you wrote is always in the game, marked "as written", and it is what happens when the player follows the story's own path. Other endings open when the player chooses differently. Where your text has no ending yet, every ending listed is a proposal and none is marked.

**Every game plays in three layers.**

| Layer | How it progresses | What the player does | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3D | Through space, in depth | Moves and looks freely through a scene | A cabin, a deck, a cavern |
| 2D | Through space, on a plane | Moves or tilts along X and Y while the world moves forward | Tilting an eye at a peephole, travelling between scenes like a side-scrolling level |
| 1D | By objective, with no travel | Searches, solves or makes something on a flat page; the play is mental | Searching a map, picking a lock, cards, board games, crafting, cooking |

Both definitions are yours. The test I have used to sort every item: in 2D the player is getting somewhere, and in 1D the player is working something out.

## 1. The Stuffed Ones

You try to survive the cabin on Ridgeway Road as a guest, fail, and wake on the shelf as one of the things that hunts the next guests.

### Story

The game keeps the novel's three movements.

1. **The Cabin.** Five friends rent a cheap cabin near the hiking trails from two old women, Mabel and Ruth. The shelves are full of handmade stuffed animals. Over three nights the wrongness stacks up: dead silence at 2:30 a.m., a metallic smell under the cedar, a bear that is "alive warm", the keepers' terrier hanging from a branch, emptied wildlife by the porch, toys found where nobody left them. On the third night a bear turns its head. The toys herd the friends through the trapdoor into a labyrinth, and the ritual room at its centre shows them what they are: "the next filling". Nobody escapes.
2. **The Hunger.** You wake with button eyes, seeing "through plastic". The keepers admire your seams. Three new guests arrive and the hunger starts. You fight it while your name and your friends fade into "cotton and thread". Jamie slips first. Then, one night, all five of you climb down.
3. **The Hunt.** The next group arrives and this time you play willingly. Five games, in order: Sound, Movement, Scent, Proximity, the Chase. It ends at the trapdoor. New stuffed ones sit on the shelf beside you, and car doors slam outside.

### Gameplay

- **Guest half.** First-person, three days and nights. You find each wrong thing and choose an explanation to shelve it. The next incident breaks that explanation. How much of the hand-sewn book you read before the ritual room decides which endings are open to you, and it carries into the second half.
- **Stuffed half.** A low camera with blurred edges. The one hard rule: you move only when no guest is looking.
- **The five games are your tools**, unlocked one per night. Sound draws a guest out of a room. Movement lets you change position between glances. Scent raises fear in a whole room. Proximity places you by a bed or a door. The Chase herds guests toward the basement and never pounces.
- **Two gauges.** Each guest has Dread. Too little and they will not break. Too much too early and they try to leave before the cabin seals on the third morning. You have Hunger. Holding still keeps your memories. Holding still too long makes you lurch at the wrong moment, as Jamie does when he falls off the shelf.
- **The loop.** Every finished hunt puts that group on the shelf for the next run. It could also be played as three guests against up to five stuffed ones.

### World script

- **Place.** The cabin: shelves, kitchen, stairs, hallway, bedrooms, trapdoor. Below it, the labyrinth: wallpapered halls, rooms of broken toys, carpet that swallows footsteps, doors that lead back to the start, warmer the deeper you go. At the centre, the ritual room: symbols stitched into the walls, a circle of torn fabric skins, a book of hand-sewn pages, an altar built from children's toys.
- **Rules.** The stuffed ones are summoned, not made. Their warmth shows how well they have fed. The forest goes silent when they are hungry. What they feed on is left emptied, not torn. The ritual begins the moment a guest steps inside. The stuffed share one pulse.
- **Cast.** The unnamed narrator. Jamie, who becomes a bear. Lila, who becomes a rabbit. Marcus and Dani. Mabel and Ruth, the keepers, who "moved slowly, like they were afraid of breaking".
- **Lines.** "They like company." "Don't move them." "They get hungry." "Strong seams. They'll last a long time." "And now… so do you."
- **Added.** The Dread and Hunger gauges, the memory loss as a system, and the multiplayer option. The text never says what Mabel and Ruth gain, and I have left that unsaid.

### Endings

- **The Cycle (as written).** You play all five games and herd the guests to the trapdoor. New stuffed ones sit beside you and car doors slam outside.
- **The Empty Toy.** You refuse to move through every night of the hunt. The hunger takes what is left of you. The others hunt without you, and you stay on the shelf, awake and still.
- **The Warning.** You keep enough of your memory, and you use Sound and Movement to frighten the guests out before the cabin seals on the third morning. They leave alive. Mabel and Ruth unpick your seams, and the cabin is listed again.
- **The Keeper.** As a guest you read the whole hand-sewn book. At the altar you may stitch instead of being stitched. Your four friends go on the shelf, and you greet the next car with "They like company."

### Scenes and screens

- **3D.** The cabin by day and by night. The porch and the silent forest edge. The labyrinth. The ritual room. The same cabin again from shelf height, as a stuffed one.
- **2D.** The cabin at night as a dollhouse cutaway, every room visible at once, where you move the stuffed ones between the guests' sweeping lines of sight. The labyrinth from above during the Chase, as you herd the guests toward its centre.
- **1D, the hand-sewn book.** Loose pages you put in order and stitch together. A finished spread teaches one part of the ritual.
- **1D, the labyrinth map.** A page you draw yourself as a guest. Doors that loop back force you to redraw it.
- **1D, the bedroom lock.** A lock-picking game played with soft fabric hands and a button on a thread, to open a guest's door from outside.
- **1D, the card table.** The friends play cards on the first night. As a stuffed one you can swap a card in the next group's game, so the deck comes out wrong and the Dread rises.

## 2. Behind the Peephole

You watch six hotel doors at night, never see what the guests did, and work it out from how they come back.

### Story

Your pitch gives the premise, the kinds of guest and the final turn. The plot between them is mine, so judge it as a proposal.

1. **Nights one and two.** You start a night job in a service corridor behind one floor of the hotel. Each door has a peephole that looks in. Your only instruction is to log returns. Six guests come back late, one of each kind you name: a mercenary, a whistleblower, an art thief, an undercover journalist, a hacker and an activist.
2. **Nights three to five.** The stories start to overlap. The suitcase that "wasn't there yesterday" in the thief's room is what the mercenary was sent to recover. The journalist's notes are about the whistleblower. The hacker's call that cuts short was to the activist. A stranger knocks on one door each night at 2:14 a.m.
3. **Night six.** Every booking traces to the same hand. "They're not here by accident."
4. **Night seven.** The 2:14 knock comes at your door. Your log was the product all along, and there is a lens in your own door. You can keep logging or walk out. Either way, a new watcher starts the next shift.

### Gameplay

- **One fixed view.** You stand at a peephole and tilt your eye to shift what the small hole shows of the room. There are six doors and only so many hours, so choosing one door means missing another.
- **Seven kinds of evidence**, taken straight from your list: clothing, phone calls, hands, luggage, visitors, the television, and rituals.
- **The morning log.** For each guest you fill in a statement from what you collected: who did what, to whom, where. A correct set locks in and opens the next night's links.
- **The mosaic board.** You draw links between guests as their stories rhyme. The board is how the player sees the convergence.
- **Being noticed.** Watch one door too long and the guest looks back. A guest who suspects changes their ritual and starts leaving false signs.
- **The rule the game never breaks.** You never leave the corridor and you never see a mission.
- **Length.** Three to four hours over seven nights. Replay value comes from the doors you did not watch.

### World script

- **Place.** One hotel, "a crossroads for people who live on the edge". One floor, one service corridor, six rooms, late at night.
- **Rules.** Everything is told in the aftermath. Small details are the whole story. Patterns matter more than events.
- **The peephole's four roles**, from your pitch, used as the four acts: a threshold on the first nights, a surveillance device as patterns build, a confession booth when guests think they are alone, and a trap on the last night.
- **Cast.** Six guests, known by trade and room number until the player earns a name. The stranger at 2:14. Whoever hired you.
- **Lines.** "You never see their missions. You only see their return." "They're not here by accident. They're not alone. And someone is watching them all."
- **Added.** The player's job, how the six guests connect, the seven-night structure and the ending. Your pitch says someone is watching them all, and I made that someone partly the player.

### Endings

Your pitch has no ending yet, so none of these is marked as written.

- **The Next Watcher.** You keep logging through night seven. Your work is accepted, and a new watcher starts the next shift at a door that looks in on you.
- **The Open Door.** You answer the 2:14 knock and walk out. Nobody logs the last night, and one guest does not come back.
- **Under the Door.** You slide your log pages under the six doors. The guests learn they were gathered on purpose and leave together before dawn. You are left alone in the corridor when the knock comes.
- **The Wrong Picture.** Your deductions are mostly wrong and you hand them in. The story you built is believed, and a guest who did nothing pays for it.

### Scenes and screens

- **3D.** The service corridor. Your own office. The stairwell and the back of house. The lobby at dawn on the last morning.
- **2D, the peephole.** The core of the game. You tilt the view up, down and across, as an eye moves at a small hole, and the room slides past the opening. A guest walks out of frame and you follow by tilting. What you need is always just past the edge.
- **2D, the corridor run.** Moving between doors is played side-on, like a level along X and Y. You have to reach a door before its guest does, past a maid's trolley, a lit window and a floorboard that creaks.
- **2D, the light under the door.** A low strip you slide along, where feet and shadows play out what the lens cannot reach.
- **1D, the morning logbook.** A page where you build each guest's statement from the pieces you collected.
- **1D, the mosaic board.** A pin board where you link guests with thread.
- **1D, the drawer lock.** A lock-picking game on the desk drawer that holds the booking ledger, opened on night six.
- **1D, the solitaire deck.** A card game for the quiet hours. Each night someone has slipped extra cards into the deck, and they carry messages.

## 3. Missing Campsites

A campground erases itself a few sites at a time, and you are one of the only people who can tell.

### Story

1. **Arrival.** A group of campers pulls into a national forest for a quiet weekend. The first sites, the ones that should be right at the entrance, are not there. "Not destroyed. Not taped off. Not overgrown." The map shows them and the signs point to them. Rangers, families and hikers act as if nothing is missing.
2. **The second morning.** More sites are gone. Nobody else remembers them. Because the loss began at the entrance and moved inward, the campers can see where it will reach next.
3. **The second evening.** The whole campground is gone except the last five sites, the ones the campers occupy. They are "the only fixed points in a landscape that's quietly unraveling".
4. **The final morning.** The forest has changed. The sky is wrong, the air is heavier, the trees stand in patterns that should not exist, and the phones show no signal, no time and no date. They did not lose the campsites. They were being pulled across, piece by piece, since they arrived.
5. **Ending (added).** You walk the loop road on the other side and find the entrance. A car pulls in. To its passengers the first sites are right there, and you are the one who acts as if nothing is missing.

### Gameplay

- **Walk and compare.** First-person, no combat. Each day you walk the loop road with a paper map and check it against the signposts and the ground.
- **Your notebook is the only thing that holds.** The game erases quietly, and that includes its own interface: entries in the map legend, photos you took, lines other characters wrote. What you wrote by hand stays. The player has to trust their own notes over the screen.
- **Asking people.** Rangers and families answer kindly and consistently, and cannot notice. Ask about site 3 and the ranger says the numbering has always started at 4. Each conversation is a reading of how far the loss has spread.
- **Fixed points.** Your five sites hold. What you carry inside them before dark also holds. You have limited room, so you choose: a signpost, the ranger's logbook, a stranger's child's bicycle. Whether to bring a person in is the hardest choice in the game.
- **Campfire.** Each night the group argues over what it believes. This is where the player commits to a theory for the next day.
- **The other side.** No clock, no map. You find your way by the patterns in the trees.
- **Length.** Two to three hours.

### World script

- **Place.** A remote campground on a single loop road, entrance sites first, the campers' five sites at the far end.
- **Rules.** The erasure is silent. It takes the memory of a thing along with the thing. It leaves paper traces, such as maps and signs, a little longer than the land. Only the campers remember.
- **Tone.** Your own three words set it: "Not dangerous. Not dramatic. Just wrong." Nothing chases the player at any point.
- **Cast.** The campers, unnamed in your pitch. The ranger at the booth. Families and hikers who come and go.
- **Lines.** "They just don't exist." "No one notices. No one cares. No one even remembers they were there."
- **Added.** The notebook, the choice of what to carry into the fixed points, the campfire and the ending. I have not named the campers or said what is on the other side.

### Endings

- **Slipped Through (as written).** You stay at your sites and keep watching. On the final morning the sky is wrong and the phones show no time. The arriving car in step 5 is this ending's last image.
- **The One Who Left.** You drive out alone on the second day, before the loss reaches the far end. You get home. Nobody remembers the others or the campground, and your notebook is the only proof they existed.
- **Held.** You carry enough into the five sites: signposts, the ranger's logbook, people. The sites hold as an island and stay in this world. Everyone you brought inside is safe, and everyone you left out is gone.
- **Nothing Missing.** You stop writing and start accepting the rangers' answers. You forget too. The weekend ends pleasantly, and there are fewer of you in the car going home.

### Scenes and screens

- **3D.** The entrance and the ranger booth. The loop road, with ground where sites used to be. Your five sites and the campfire. The forest on the other side.
- **2D.** The loop road from above at dusk, walked by torchlight, where the gaps show as bare ground between numbered posts. The other side from above, where the trees form patterns you can only read from that height.
- **1D, the paper map.** A page you mark up and check against the ground. Its legend loses entries overnight.
- **1D, the notebook.** Your own pages, the only ones that hold.
- **1D, the bear-box lock.** A combination lock on the ranger's steel box, which you open to take the logbook into your site.
- **1D, the campfire card game.** The group plays each night. The deck is a few cards short each time, and a hand is dealt to a seat nobody remembers filling.
- **1D, the family's board game.** A family lends you a board game. Each day it has fewer pieces and fewer squares, and they play on without noticing.

## 4. The Redacted Testament of Lira Calyx

You are a field scientist cataloguing a remade world, and your own journal is being corrected, censored and falsified as you write it.

### Story

Your four books become four acts, and the final chapter becomes a playable epilogue.

1. **Biology of the Modern World.** Dr. Lira Calyx sets up on a Mid-Slope research terrace and re-establishes baselines. On the third night the Spore-Weavers pulse across a valley like one organism. She names it Pattern α. A note in another hand appears on page 47 of her journal. Ember Wolf juveniles turn up too high, Aether-Vines lean toward one point on the Peaks, and a storm leaves her a crystalline fragment. She forms Hypothesis 1 and gets a threat from a corporate patron.
2. **The Aetheric Systems.** The fragment leads into archives and labs. She finds signs of deep-earth seeding, a scrap that reads "She's close. Move the logs.", and the names Dr. Solace Veylor and Operation ReGenesis. Her brief is redacted and she is branded a saboteur.
3. **The Human Threshold.** She travels among Peakborn, Midlanders and Lowland Remnants and finds their songs and chants carry real measurements. She reaches a subterranean city, is betrayed by an engineer she trusted, and escapes with proof of the Second Genesis: a plan to destabilize the Peaks and force a reset.
4. **Final Reckoning.** With Mara, Joren and Kade she descends to the Continuum's core and fires her phase-offset pulses. Veylor had seeded false data years earlier. The result is "a fragile pause and a partial fracture". Lira is taken into the mountain.
5. **Epilogue.** You play the archivist who left the margin notes, arrived too late, and stole the books.

### Gameplay

- **Survey.** You carry instruments and record light spectra, spore density and migration counts. Anomalies appear when a reading breaks a baseline you set yourself. Linking observations forms a hypothesis and opens new ground.
- **The journal is the interface, and it is not safe.** Margin notes in another hand act as hints. After you publish, redactions black out pages you rely on. In Act 3 some entries are overwritten with false readings, and you have to re-check your own notes.
- **Climbing is progress.** Lowlands, Mid-Slopes, High Peaks. Gear and tolerance for Aetheric exposure decide how high you can work.
- **Myth as data.** You learn Peakborn storm-songs and Midlander harvest chants as patterns, then match them against your readings to locate nodes.
- **Infiltration.** Short stealth sections in labs and Continuum outposts, built around taking logs, not fighting.
- **The final puzzle.** You place emitters on a network of nodes and set their timing. Some inputs are corrupted. Only what you verified by hand in earlier acts can be trusted, so careful play saves more terraces. It never saves everything.
- **Epilogue.** You assemble the testament and decide what to restore and what stays blacked out.

### World script

- **The Convergence Era.** A nuclear cataclysm folded the crust into megapeaks tall enough to make their own weather and gravity anomalies. Fallout fused with deep minerals into Aetheric Compounds, which became currency.
- **Three realms.** The Lowlands, the Scorched Biome: radiation pockets, crystalline-bark flora, chemical storms. The Mid-Slopes, the Verdant Reclamation Zone: forests, glowing rivers, cliffside terraces. The High Peaks, the Aetheric Frontier: thin air, floating spores, low-gravity creatures, storms inside the rock.
- **Peoples and powers.** Peakborn, Midlanders, Lowland Remnants. The Council of Peaks. The Continuum, under Veylor, who believes "a hard reset is a mercy".
- **Creatures.** Spore-Weavers, Ridgeback Striders, Ember Wolves, Aether-Vines.
- **Lines.** "Life is not choosing upward. It is being chosen." "You are looking at the wrong end of the needle. Look below." "This is not paranoia. This is presence." "If you are reading this, you are a target."
- **Added.** The instruments as play, the verification rule in the final puzzle and the playable epilogue. The story and its ending are yours, unchanged.

### Endings

- **The Redacted Testament (as written).** You fire the pulses on partly corrupted data. The result is the fragile pause. Lira is taken, and the archivist carries the books out.
- **The Archivist in Time.** Through all four acts you act on the margin notes and write replies beside them. The follower reaches Lira before the tunnels seal. She lives in hiding, the testament is published under her own name, and the Continuum hunts them both.
- **Directed Resilience.** In the core you accept Veylor's argument and do not fire. The Second Genesis runs. The Peaks reset, and Lira becomes the cataloguer of the world that follows, with her name on it.
- **Fire.** You give up on phase offsets and destroy the core outright, against her own plea of "phase offsets, not fire". The Continuum falls, and the Mid-Slope terraces fall with it.

### Scenes and screens

- **3D.** The Mid-Slope terrace and the valley of the Spore-Weavers. The Lowlands. The High Peaks. The archive and the corporate lab. The subterranean city and the core beneath Peak 12.
- **2D.** The mountain in cross-section, where you plan and make each climb side-on from Lowlands to Peaks. The fragment under the microscope, where you steer through its micro-filaments from above.
- **1D, the journal.** Pages you write, which gain margin notes and black bars, and which you later check for forgeries.
- **1D, the specimen cards.** A card game of sorting and matching creatures by trait, which is how hypotheses are formed.
- **1D, the archive lock.** A cipher lock on the sealed Continuum archive, solved with patterns from the storm-songs.
- **1D, the node board.** A board game for the finale. You place emitters on a map of nodes, and Veylor has already placed hidden pieces of his own.

## 5. WrymWretch

Six elves and their twin-soul dragons cross a world that is forgetting itself, and what the world forgets stops existing.

### Story

1. **Act I, the six signs.** Each hero meets the memory-plague alone. Thalen finds a tree split from within and says "The forest is… forgetting." Aeris watches the Sky Elves sicken as the wind whispers "Remember me." Rhyla sees the Heartstone dim. Lunessa finds a monolith whose symbols her mind will not hold. Kael reads the prophecy: "When the last memory fades, the Astral Architect shall rise." Seraphyne sees the Sigil of Izladar in the stars. The first seal cracks.
2. **Act II, the awakening.** Each elf's dragon wakes and the bond locks in. All six pairs turn toward #Wrymreach, the birthplace of their kind. A voice in the chasm says "I… remember… me…" The second seal cracks.
3. **Act III (added, and yours to decide).** Your text stops at Act II. The codex gives the problem a clear shape: Shallock Izladar's prison is sealed by memory, and the Remnants erase all memory of him to break it. So the heroes cannot win by destroying him or by forgetting him. Someone has to keep remembering. I suggest the last act turns on who carries that, and at what cost.

The same file holds two prose chapters about Alexi, a hungover sailor, a princess and the Lord of Tea. They are a separate story, The Lord of the Sea, built out as its own game in section 10.

### Gameplay

- **Six openings, then one party.** Each pair gets a short chapter in its own realm with its own way of playing, before the paths meet.
- **One unit, two halves.** Each hero is an elf and a dragon sharing one life: "if memory dies… so do we". You switch between them or act together. Acting together strengthens spells and senses, and shared dreams work as scouting.
- **Forgetting is the threat.** As memory fades, regions lose their names on the map, people forget what they asked of you, bestiary pages blank out, and abilities lose their names and then their effect.
- **Remembering is the answer.** You restore things by recording them at crystals, retelling them to others and re-drawing constellations. The Remnants kill witnesses, so many missions are about keeping one person alive who remembers.
- **Puzzles you cannot write down.** Like Lunessa's monolith, some symbols slip from the player's own notes, and have to be solved by pattern.
- **Battles.** Turn-based, built around pairing the six dragons' powers.

### World script

- **Origin.** The First Light shattered into the Sun's Roaring Anger and the Moon's Silent Tear. Dragons were born of the fury and elves of the sorrow, joined by the Threefold Bond.
- **Magic.** Memory shapes reality. When memory fades, reality decays.
- **Places.** Six realms around #Wrymreach, "a cosmic wound" where time folds and memory bleeds. Beyond them, the Astral Expanse, Izladar's prison.
- **Factions.** Luminary Priory, Duskbane Syndicate, Remnants of Izladar, Wyrmguard, Circle of Seasons, Tidecallers, Stonekeepers, Galechanters.

| Tribe | Realm | Hero | Dragon | Dragon's power |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wood Elves, Cedarheart Enclave | West, Cedarheart Forest | Thalen | Sylvan | Shapes plants, heals forests |
| Sky Elves, Galewing Assembly | North, Galewing Peaks | Aeris Galewing | Aetherius | Shapes winds, shares thought |
| Earth Elves, Mosswhisper Tribe | South, Mosswhisper Depths | Rhyla Mosswhisper | Gaia | Shapes stone, recalls fossil memory |
| Water Elves, Tidecaller Circle | East, Tidecaller Shores | Lunessa Tidecaller | Aquatica | Shapes tides, purifies seas |
| Dark Elves, Duskbane Syndicate | Below, Duskbane Underworld | Kael Duskbane | Umbra | Shadow, illusion, the hunt |
| Celestial Elves, Luminary Priory | Above, the High Realm | Seraphyne | Astraia | Astral shields, beams, glimpses of futures |

- **Added.** The forgetting and remembering systems, the unwritable puzzles, and the Act III suggestion. Everything in the table is from your codex.

### Endings

Your text stops at Act II, so none of these is marked as written.

- **The Rememberer.** One pair stays at #Wrymreach to hold Izladar's name for good. The seal holds and the world's memory returns. The player chooses which pair.
- **The Architect Rises.** You let too much be forgotten on the way. The last memory fades, the Remnants are proved right or wrong, and Shallock Izladar steps out of the Astral Expanse.
- **Remembered Whole.** You restore enough of the world's memory to learn who Izladar was before he ascended: a Celestial Elf scholar. The voice that says "I… remember… me…" finishes the sentence. He chooses to stay, and the prison no longer needs a guard.
- **Dormant Again.** The six pairs seal the wound by giving up their bonds. The world is saved, and the elves and dragons forget each other, as they had before the story began.

### Scenes and screens

- **3D.** Cedarheart Forest. The sky-cities of Galewing Peaks. The Heartstone Cavern in Mosswhisper Depths. The Tidecaller Shores and the seabed monolith. The Duskbane Underworld. The High Realm. The chasm of #Wrymreach.
- **2D.** Flight on dragonback, side-on, as a silhouette against the sky-cities. Lunessa's dive to the monolith, side-on through darkening water. Kael's hunt through the Underworld tunnels, seen from above.
- **1D, the world map.** A page that loses its names as places are forgotten, and which you re-letter by hand.
- **1D, the constellation chart.** A page of stars where you re-draw the lines the sky has lost.
- **1D, the memory cards.** A matching card game whose faces blank out as you play, used to restore a bestiary page or a person's name.
- **1D, the prophecy lock.** A rune lock on the door of the forbidden chamber, which Kael picks.
- **1D, the board of six realms.** A board game the elves play, with one side for each tribe. Winning a match against a tribe's elder earns that tribe's trust.

## 6. Project: Man Eater

You are posted to a base with three rules, and the food that keeps you strong enough to break them is what is changing you.

### Story

1. **Arrival.** Agent Fisk reports to Project Man Eater in the woods and mountains of North Dakota. The base is armoured "not to keep things out, but instead to keep them in". The new barracks are already run down. The crew look sick and thin. There are claw marks on the walls, and it is colder inside than out. Fisk has seen worse postings and shelves each thing.
2. **The rules.** Sam, who runs the project, walks him down halls four feet wide and fifteen feet high and gives three rules: eat only what the cafeteria serves, never leave the room after 10:00, never leave the base.
3. **First night.** At 10:00 a low, flute-like groaning calls. His bunkmate gets up and walks out. Fisk does not stop him, and finds he cannot feel sad about it.
4. **The meal.** The meat makes him hungrier the more he eats. By afternoon he can feel his ribs. Sam says the bunkmate was "transferred".
5. **Second night.** Fisk and his new bunkmate Jax follow the sound and find a pale giant playing the flute, who says "Beware the Windigo, for I am he. Follow in my footsteps, and you will become me." They are gassed from behind.
6. **The cave.** Sam explains the project: feed recruits flesh and chemicals to bring on the curse quickly, then study them. Fisk wakes transformed, a tube in his arm, "like an animal in a zoo".

The game stretches the two nights to about a week of the same pattern. The written ending is one of several.

### Gameplay

- **Days and nights.** First-person. By day you do duties, eat and talk. After 10:00 you stay in your bunk or go out.
- **The diet.** Eating the ration makes you stronger, faster and able to bear the cold. It also raises your hunger, thins your body and dulls your mind, which the game shows by simplifying its own text and prompts. Refusing keeps you clear-headed and too weak to get far. Bread quiets the hunger for a while.
- **The call.** At 10:00 the flute pulls at you. Following it is easy. So is not caring: the game removes the options to object, as Fisk's own feelings are removed.
- **The list.** Each wrong thing goes into a list with Fisk's explanation beside it. The next discovery breaks that explanation.
- **The building fits you later.** Doors and halls are far too tall on day one. As you change, the camera rises until they are the right size.
- **Hand-off (added).** Your next playthrough starts as a new recruit. Your earlier notes are hidden in the bunk, and the flute down the hall is you.

### World script

- **Place.** Barracks, cafeteria, the tall narrow halls, your bunk with its clock, and a cave under the base with stalagmites.
- **Rules.** The base says it is the first recorded birthplace of a Windigo. The curse comes from eating human flesh. The project brings it on with the cafeteria meat. A transformed man keeps his hatred and loses the ability to act on it.
- **Cast.** Fisk. Sam, who drops the formalities and smiles. The first bunkmate, never named. Jax. The Windigo.
- **Lines.** "You can just call me Sam." "Do not, under any circumstances, leave this room after 10:00 precisely." "We won't make the same mistakes as we have with the rest."
- **Links to Who Did It.** Your Grounds 99 material already points at a North Dakota site and names this story as a special ending. This could be a spin-off in that world.
- **Care needed.** The Windigo comes from the traditions of Algonquian-speaking peoples, and Sam's account of it is rough. I would keep the horror aimed at the institution and get the folklore reviewed before building on it.
- **Added.** The longer week, the diet and call systems, the rising camera and the hand-off.

### Endings

- **The Zoo (as written).** You eat, you follow the flute, and you are taken to the cave. You wake transformed, a test subject who will never see daylight.
- **Rule Three.** You break the last rule and go over the perimeter in winter. Only someone who has eaten enough can survive the cold, so you get out already changing. Fisk's own thought comes true: "this would only spread".
- **Transferred.** You refuse the meat from the first day and never follow the sound. You grow too weak to work. Sam tells your bunkmate you were transferred, and you never learn what the base was.
- **Call Me Sam.** You report Jax and make yourself useful. You are kept on as staff. The game ends with you walking a new recruit down the tall halls and giving him the three rules.

### Scenes and screens

- **3D.** The barracks and the tall, narrow halls. The cafeteria. Your bunk. The halls after 10:00, toward the flute. The cave. The perimeter in snow.
- **2D.** The tall halls at night, side-on and in silhouette, where your own shadow grows longer each night. The perimeter crossing from above, where your footprints in the snow are what the guards follow.
- **1D, the bunk door lock.** A lock-picking game to get out after 10:00. Your fingers are colder and thinner each night, which changes how it handles.
- **1D, the barracks card game.** The crew plays for bread. There are fewer chairs at the table each night, and nobody mentions it.
- **1D, the floor plan.** A page you draw of the base, room by room.
- **1D, the medical card.** A page of measurements that changes day by day, beside the list of wrong things and Fisk's explanations.

## 7. A Journey Into the Heartstream

A short co-op game in which friends ride a raft down a river of blood toward a heart that will separate them, and nothing they do can prevent it.

### Story

1. **The marrow.** The Blue-Born grow up in the marrow caverns of the bones. Their skin is "the color of uncirculated blood". They carve tools together, sing marrow-songs and dream of the world above the bone.
2. **The Recognition.** At adulthood their blue begins to fade to red, "the body's way of calling them upward". The elders gather, the marrow lights dim, and the young men stand together.
3. **The ascent.** A shaft opens in the bone. They climb as the heat rises and the pulse grows louder.
4. **The river.** They come out into the Bloodstream, which is "not a tube" but a river. They board the Ceremonial Raft, "still a brotherhood". Artery walls rise like canyon cliffs. They "laugh, argue, dream, and fear".
5. **The heart.** The heart is "a cathedral". The current splits. One by one they are swept into different chambers. "There is no time for goodbye."
6. **After (added).** Each player rides a short last stretch alone, turning fully red.

### Gameplay

- **Two to four players, sixty to ninety minutes, played once.** A solo mode gives you computer-controlled brothers.
- **The marrow is where you get attached.** You carve a tool for another player and they carve one for you. You sing together by keeping time with the distant pulse. None of this is a test. It exists so that there is something to lose.
- **The climb.** A simple shared ascent where you pull each other up.
- **The raft.** Everyone steers together. The current surges with each heartbeat, so you row on the beat. You avoid eddies, stinging tendrils and cell-shaped predators, and you hide from the large immune beasts. Nobody fights. Between hazards the river goes calm and the game leaves room to talk.
- **The heart.** Each player gets a control for holding on to the others. It almost always fails. Grips fail one at a time, and each player's voice is cut from the chat as they go.
- **Alone.** You keep one thing: the tool the other player carved for you.

### World script

- **The marrow caverns.** Glowing tunnels whose walls pulse with the distant heart. A society that is "calm, cold, and ceremonial", in which everything prepares for leaving.
- **Colour is the law of this world.** Blue is youth before recognition. Red is circulation. Nobody chooses when they change.
- **The Bloodstream.** A wide crimson river "like the Amazon", with humid, metallic air. Branching vessels like river deltas. Translucent predators, drifting tendrils, immune beasts on patrol.
- **The raft.** A living vessel, grown from tissue and shaped by tradition.
- **The heart.** A thundering structure of muscle and light.
- **Lines.** "No one stays together. Everyone is carried away. Life is a river that never lets you hold what you love for long."
- **Added.** The carved tools, rowing on the beat, the grip that fails, the voice cut and the solo ending. I gave the Blue-Born no names and added no reunion.

### Endings

- **Carried Away (as written).** The current splits in the heart and every player is swept a different way. "No one stays together."
- **Two Together.** Two players who rowed in time for the whole river, and who both hold on at the same beat, are swept into the same chamber. The others are lost to them. It is rare, and the game never says it is possible.
- **The One Who Stayed.** At the Recognition a player refuses the climb. They stay in the marrow, keep their blue a while longer, and become the elder who dims the lights for the next group.
- **Taken by the River.** The raft is steered badly through the patrols. An immune beast takes one of you before the heart, and the rest arrive with an empty place on the raft.

### Scenes and screens

- **3D.** The marrow caverns. The shaft through the bone. The river between artery walls. The delta of branching vessels. The heart. The narrow vessel each player ends in alone.
- **2D.** The climb up the shaft, side-on, with the players as small figures pulling each other up. Stretches of the river in cross-section, the raft on the surface and the predators visible below it.
- **1D, the carving bench.** A page where you cut a tool for another player, stroke by stroke.
- **1D, the river chart.** A map page of branching vessels, where the raft votes on a route.
- **1D, the marrow game.** A board game of bone counters that the Blue-Born play in the caverns. The players start a match before the Recognition and never finish it.
- **1D, the seal on the shaft.** A lock that only opens when every player turns their piece at the same moment.

## 8. People of the Bark

You run a tiny civilization on the bark of a tree, where a squirrel is a cataclysm and a human hand is the arrival of a god.

### Story

The story is one year on the Endless Trunk, told as a run of omens. Your treatment gives the first and the last. The middle ones come from your list of what the bark people believe.

1. **The Tremor.** At dawn the bark shakes. Dust falls from the ridges, the elders declare omens, the warriors run to the battlements and the scholars record the pattern. It is a squirrel scratching an itch.
2. **The seasons.** Rain comes as a blessing. Windstorms come as divine trials. Birdsong is read as prophecy. Leaves fall, and the people mourn, because falling leaves are omens of death. Insects arrive as monsters or as gods, depending on their size.
3. **The Coming of the Shadow.** A shadow covers the whole world. The priests chant and the warriors prepare for war. It is a human hand, "someone checking for termites".
4. **The pull-back.** The view rises until the Endless Trunk is "just a tree in someone's backyard". The game ends on your moral: "Even the smallest world is a universe to the ones who live in it."

### Gameplay

- **Build on a wall.** The map is a vertical sheet of bark. Cities cling to ridges and grooves, roads run along cracks, temples are carved into knots and watchtowers rise from splinters.
- **Two views.** At any time you can pull back and see the backyard: the squirrel, the rain cloud, the hand. Zoomed in, the same event is an epic. Switching between them is the joke, and by the end it is the point.
- **Three institutions.** Elders read omens, warriors man the battlements, scholars keep records. You decide how much of your people's effort goes to each.
- **Reading an event.** After each event you choose what it meant: trial, blessing, prophecy or message. The choice sets policy and belief for the season.
- **Belief that half works.** Your text says of their faith "They're not wrong. But they're not right either." In play, scholars who record every tremor start predicting the squirrel's routine without knowing what a squirrel is.
- **Sap** is sacred water and the main resource.
- **No victory.** You carry a people through a year and face the Shadow with the culture you built.

### World script

- **The world.** The outer rind of a tree. "Even a single inch of bark is an entire country to them." The tree is "the sky, the ground, the history, the future".
- **The people.** "Small, agile, and deeply spiritual." They believe the tree is alive and conscious. It is alive. It is also just a tree.
- **Rules.** Nothing outside the tree is ever explained to the bark people. Every disaster has an ordinary cause the player can see and they cannot. Their courage is never the punchline.
- **Tone.** "Epic stakes inside a world that is cosmically tiny."
- **Cast.** Elders, young warriors, scholars and priests. Your treatment names no individuals.
- **Lines.** "Their lives still matter. Their stories still matter. Their courage still matters."
- **Added.** The two views, the three-way split of effort, the reading of events and sap as a resource. Your treatment does not say what the hand does next, so the game ends before it does anything.

### Endings

- **The Shadow Passes (as written).** You keep the three institutions in balance. The priests chant, the warriors stand ready, the hand checks for termites and goes away. The view pulls back to the backyard, and their lives still matter.
- **The Edge of the World.** You favour the scholars. Their tremor records show the Shadow is a thing with a shape and a habit. One of them draws the first map with an edge on it, and the people argue over whether it is heresy.
- **The War on the God.** You favour the warriors. They attack the hand. From the backyard, someone gets a splinter and pulls away. On the bark it is the greatest victory in history.
- **The Flake.** You tap too much sap and carve too deep into the knots. A plate of bark comes loose. In the backyard a flake falls from a tree and nobody looks up.

### Scenes and screens

- **3D.** The bark city on its ridges and grooves, where you build. The inside of a temple carved into a knot. The battlements during a tremor. An expedition out along a branch. The backyard, seen in the pull-back.
- **2D, the runner.** Travel between cities is played side-on. A messenger runs a crack in the bark like a level along X and Y, over splinters, sap and falling dust, to carry an omen before the next tremor.
- **2D, the crack defence.** Warriors hold a crack against insects, side-on along its length, falling back from one barricade to the next.
- **1D, the city plan.** A map page of the settled bark, which you search for where to build next.
- **1D, the omen deck.** A card game. After each event the elders lay out cards, and the spread you choose decides what the event meant.
- **1D, the tremor scroll.** A page of every tremor recorded, where the scholars match patterns to predict the next one.
- **1D, the knot door.** A lock made of growth rings, which you turn into line to open a temple.
- **1D, the sap kitchen.** A cooking game. Sap is boiled and mixed into food, sealant or offerings, and the recipe decides which.
- **1D, the game of Trunk and Shadow.** A board game the bark people play about their own world. Its rules change as their beliefs do.

## 9. The Endless Temple

You are the keeper of a library where every book is a living universe, and you look after those worlds by reading yourself into them.

### Story

1. **The keeper.** Elaris Sel Marden is an elf scholar, nearly eight feet tall, pale green, in monk's attire. He is bound to the temple for life and can only be replaced when he dies. He did not choose it. He calls it "an honor and a much better outcome than he deserved", and the text does not say why.
2. **The duty.** The temple exists "to protect and oversee the countless worlds safely stowed within". Each book holds a universe with its own myths, and those hold smaller worlds of their own.
3. **The first book.** Elaris picks up a plain book with sand sifting from its pages and no name on it. He decides to read it "and find out what to call it". He brews a mint tea that lets a scholar see a book as if he were its narrator.
4. **Inside.** He becomes Xandri, son of the great explorer who died looking for the end of the desert. Xandri asks why nobody rides the Xandrixes to the end, and a shaman strikes him: "We do not ride on the backs of the gods." That night he packs a patched bag and walks into the sand sea. A wave of sand swallows him. By morning he has tied broad leaves to his feet, as his father taught him, and walks on the waves.
5. **From here (added).** Your chapter stops on the dunes. I suggest the book ends when the player gives it its name, which is the task Elaris set himself. Whether the desert has an end is yours to decide.

### Gameplay

- **The temple is the hub.** Shelves, tables, a pantry of jars and teas, a cuckoo clock. A book in trouble shows it on the outside, as the first one leaks sand.
- **Tea is how you enter.** The mint tea puts you inside a book as its narrator. Other teas could change how you read, and those would be additions.
- **Two voices.** Your chapter moves from Xandri's "I" to "he". The game uses that. While the tea holds, you play in first person. As it fades, the view pulls out to third person and your control loosens, until you return to the temple.
- **Book one, the sand sea.** You cross dunes that move like water. Leaf shoes let you walk the waves. Days burn and nights freeze, so you bury yourself in sand to sleep. The Xandrixes cross the desert at ease, and riding one is forbidden. Whether to break that rule is the book's central choice.
- **Books within books.** A world can contain a book of its own, which opens a smaller world.
- **One book per release.** The game can ship with a few books and grow.

### World script

- **The temple.** Not a place of worship. Books on shelves, floors and tables, some in the pantry beside jars of goblin's eyes. Sunlight through an open window.
- **Rules.** Every book is alive. The keeper never leaves. Reading with the tea makes the reader the narrator.
- **The first book's world.** A desert that "seems to extend on into eternity". A clan descended from lizards, who believe the Xandrixes gave them human form and can take it back. Shamans, led by Xadra, with a rule for everything. Silver dunes under the moon, purple vines, prickle bushes, and leaves shaped like the ears of the Zorlop.
- **Cast.** Elaris. Xandri. His dead father, the explorer. Xadra.
- **Lines.** "Since I am enshrined in this place for eternity, I might as well come to love what I am protecting." "I had to prove myself."
- **An option.** The Heartstream and the Bark could sit on these shelves as two more books. That link is my suggestion and is not in your text.
- **Added.** The hub, the fading tea, the choice about riding and the naming ending.

### Endings

Your chapter stops on the dunes, so none of these is marked as written. They are endings for the first book.

- **The Named Book.** Xandri walks further than his father did. Elaris gives the book its name, the sand stops sifting from its pages, and he shelves it.
- **The Rider.** Xandri rides a Xandrix. He reaches what the shamans said could not be reached, and learns whether the gods would really turn his people back into lizards. He does not come home to tell them.
- **The Son Returns.** Xandri turns back. He becomes the storyteller, and the tale told at the start is now told about him. The book stays nameless and keeps leaking sand.
- **The Reader Who Stayed.** Elaris keeps drinking the tea and does not come back out. In the temple, with nobody watching, a second book begins to leak.

### Scenes and screens

- **3D.** The temple halls. Elaris's room and the pantry. The tribe's village and the storyteller's circle. The sand sea by moonlight. The sand sea by day. The back of a Xandrix.
- **2D.** The book's own illustrations brought to life, where Xandri rides the sand waves side-on across the page. The temple stacks, side-on, as Elaris walks shelves that do not end.
- **1D, the open book.** The page itself, with sand running from the gutter. You turn it, read it, and at the end write its name.
- **1D, the tea cards.** A card game of herbs. The hand you blend decides how deeply you enter a book and how hard it is to leave.
- **1D, Xandri's map.** A page he draws as he goes, with his father's furthest point marked on it.
- **1D, the clasp locks.** Lock-picking on the sealed books of the temple, each clasp built differently.
- **1D, the rule tablet.** The shamans have a rule for everything. This is a board of their rules, where you look for the gap that lets Xandri do what he wants without breaking one.

## 10. The Lord of the Sea

You are Poseidon long after the age of the Greek gods, weak, hungover and living on an old boat, in a century when a trading company has brought peace and order to the ocean and means to own it.

### Story

The first two beats are your chapters. The premise is as you gave it to me: a weakened Poseidon in the age of maritime peace under the East India Company. The rest is my proposal.

1. **The Shore Between Worlds.** A man wakes on an old boat to bottles rolling across the cabin floor. The walls are crowded with sketches, foreign poems, maps and jars of spice and tea from ports he barely remembers. He rows a dinghy to a quiet beach and stands at the tideline, "a man on the edge of remembering something important".
2. **The Princess on the Shore.** A voice calls "Alexi!" He runs, fails to stop, and knocks a princess into the sand. She brings a warning. Her father's debts have grown too great and he has sold his halls to the Great British Tea Company. The Lord of Tea now wants "every ship, every vessel, every soul that sails". Something in Alexi older than memory knows "the sea was not meant to be owned".
3. **The ordered sea (added).** Alexi tries to raise a wave and gets a ripple. He sails out and sees what the Company has built: charted lanes, licensed ships, one flag on every mast, and no piracy, no war, almost no wrecks. The peace is real.
4. **What is left of the old sea (added).** He looks for the places and people that still treat the ocean as "a wild, living thing": sailors who still sing to the tide, waters no chart covers, creatures that remember his name better than he does.
5. **The Lord of Tea (added).** The Company cannot be sunk ship by ship. It holds the sea by debts and charters, the way it took the king's halls. Alexi has to break those, with the princess as his guide to how they work.
6. **The ending is yours.** The question the story sets up is whether a sea that cannot be owned can be ruled by a god either.

### Gameplay

- **Sailing first.** You sail a small old boat across an open chart, with a dinghy for landings. The boat's cabin is your base, and what you collect goes on its walls and shelves.
- **Your power is the sea's wildness.** In Company waters the sea is calm, safe and patrolled, and you can barely stir it. In wild waters you can raise swell, fog and current, and later storms. Each power has to be remembered before it can be used, in keeping with a man "on the edge of remembering".
- **Order against Wild.** Every region sits somewhere between the two. Pushing a region toward Wild makes you stronger there. It also brings back wrecks and drownings, and the people living under the Company's peace did not ask for that. This is the game's real decision, repeated region by region.
- **Devotion.** Crews who sing "drunken hymns to the tide" restore you. Finding them, earning them and keeping them alive is how you grow.
- **Land is hard.** Alexi is "a man who had only recently remembered he possessed legs". Ashore he is clumsy and funny. At the tideline he is at his strongest.
- **Breaking the Company.** You free a port by undoing the charter or debt that binds it: stealing a ledger, exposing a contract, carrying a ruler's payment through a blockade.

### World script

- **The age.** Long after the gods of Greece. Sail, trade and empire. A sea at peace because one company keeps it so.
- **The Company.** In your chapter it is the Great British Tea Company, led by the Lord of Tea. Your note models it on the East India Company. It turns the ocean into "a factory, a fleet, a machine".
- **Rules.** A god is as strong as what he rules is free. The sea remembers him even where he has forgotten himself. "Everything has a lord."
- **Cast.** Alexi, who is Poseidon, though your chapters never say the name. The princess, sharp and unnamed, who has "long ago accepted that her life would never be normal". Her father, the indebted king. The Lord of Tea. The narrator, who says "I—who am him and not him, who walk beside him and inside him".
- **Voice.** Comic on the surface and mythic underneath, as in your second chapter: "He performed what future scholars might generously describe as a bold and experimental interpretation of running."
- **Lines.** "Alexi, I bring a warning." "Tea has a lord." "He will drain the empire, and then the people, until all that remains is a wasteland of scraps and ash."
- **Added.** Beats 3 to 5, the Order against Wild system, devotion as power and the way ports are freed. I have not said who the narrator is. My reading is the god's older self, and I would like you to confirm or correct that.

### Endings

You have written two chapters, so none of these is marked as written.

- **The Wild Sea.** You push every region to Wild and break the Company. Alexi is Poseidon again. The hymns come back, and so do the wrecks.
- **The Lord's Peace.** You leave the order standing, or fail to break it. The Lord of Tea owns every ship that sails. The sea is safe, and Alexi is only a man on an old boat who cannot remember why the tide used to answer.
- **No Lord.** You break the Company and then give up the sea yourself, because it "was not meant to be owned" by anyone. Alexi becomes a mortal sailor, and the sea belongs to whoever is on it.
- **The Charter.** With the princess you write terms between a god and the people who sail: safe lanes kept calm, and wild water left wild. It needs a balance of Order and Wild across the chart, and both sides sign.

### Scenes and screens

- **3D.** The boat's cabin and deck. The beach and the tideline. A Company shipping lane. Uncharted wild water. A Company port. The Lord of Tea's counting-house. The deep.
- **2D.** Storms and sea fights played side-on, in the style of an engraved sea chart come to life. Dives into the deep, side-on, where Alexi moves as he cannot on land.
- **1D, the sea chart.** A map page shaded by Order and Wild, where you plan each voyage.
- **1D, the ledgers and charters.** Pages of Company paperwork, where you hunt for the clause that binds a port.
- **1D, the tavern game.** A card and dice game played with sailors. The stakes are crew, songs and news.
- **1D, the strongbox.** A lock-picking game on Company strongboxes and the counting-house door.
- **1D, the tea board.** A board game against the Lord of Tea, played over trade lanes. The final negotiation is a match on this board, and The Charter ending is a drawn game.
